Outreach playbook

This is the plan for the next two weeks. The goal is simple: book your first real classes, run them, and keep a record of what you tried and what happened.

Read the two-week plan, copy templates from the section below, and log every message in tracker.csv. Internal page for the team. It is not linked from the site.

Your next 2 weeks

Effectuation means you start with what you already have, risk only what you can afford to lose, let partners shape the plan, turn surprises into options, and check the results every week. Each step lists who does it and how you will know it worked.

Bird-in-hand: start with who you know

Use what you already have: your own groups, floors, chapters and friends.

  1. Write your starting list. Each teacher lists every sorority, club, dorm floor and friend group they belong to, and one person in each who could host or pass the idea along. Who: each teacher. Measured by: names added to tracker.csv by day 2.
  2. Message your closest groups first. Send template (g) or (h) to the five groups you know best, asking for one group of 6 or more. Who: each teacher, for their own groups. Measured by: replies and bookings in the tracker.

Affordable loss: pilot small and set the cap first

Each class costs groceries and time. Decide how much you will lose before you book anything.

  1. Set your limit. Write down the most you will spend on groceries and the most hours you will give to the first three classes. Put both in the tracker Notes. Who: Zoe. Measured by: a written cap that you check at each weekly review.
  2. Run 2 or 3 pilot classes. Offer the first one to a friend group of 6 or more at $14 a person. Hold the next ones in different apartments so you learn what changes. Who: Zoe, plus the teacher who knows each group. Measured by: classes held, people taught, and feedback forms returned.

Crazy quilt: partners who co-commit

Partners bring things you cannot get alone. Ask each one for a specific thing, and offer something back.

  1. Send the partner emails. Send template (d) to Morven Kitchen Garden, (e) to the UVA Community Food Pantry, (c) to one RA or residence life contact, (a) to one sorority philanthropy or social chair, and (b) to one CIO president. Who (suggested split): Zoe for Morven and the pantry; Lauren or Meg for residence life; Anneke for sorority chairs; Maddie for CIOs. Measured by: replies, and one next step per partner (a call, a class date, or a clear no).
  2. Know what each partner brings. Use this list to decide what to offer and what to ask for.
    • Morven Kitchen Garden (student-run garden on UVA's Morven Farm) Brings: fresh produce, if they can supply it. Not yet confirmed. You offer: a class menu built around what they grow, and credit in the class. Ask: produce for upcoming classes, or a volunteer exchange.
    • UVA Community Food Pantry Brings: the place your donations go. Confirm with them how they want to receive it. You offer: a clear line in every booking about the donation. Ask: how and how often the donation is sent, and whether they want to co-host a class.
    • RAs and residence life Brings: access to a hall and to floor groups. You offer: a class for a floor group. Ask: permission to share the idea with a floor, and any kitchen residents can use.
    • Sorority philanthropy and social chairs Brings: group bookings and a philanthropy tie-in. You offer: a sisterhood night built around cooking. Ask: a booking of 6 or more for a chapter event.
    • UVA dining, nutrition or wellness groups Brings: credibility and referrals to students. You offer: a short guide to cooking for first-years. Ask: a referral or a share of your booking link.
    • CIO presidents Brings: member lists and meeting time. You offer: a class for members. Ask: a shout-out or a few minutes at a meeting.
    Who: whoever owns the contact. Measured by: one row per partner in the tracker with a status and a next step.

Lemonade: when a class is cancelled or few people sign up

Treat a weak result as information, and turn it into the next try.

  1. Combine small lists. Two groups of 3 or 4 who both want a class can become one class of 6 or more. Who: the teacher who has both lists. Measured by: combined bookings in the tracker.
  2. Ask the people who said no. Send one question: "What would make it work for your group?" Who: Zoe. Measured by: answers in the Notes column, and at least one change you made because of them.
  3. Rebook the open date. Offer the date to the next partner on your list, such as a hall or a chapter. Who: the teacher who owns that partner. Measured by: a new class date in the tracker.
  4. Write your cancellation rule before the first booking. Put it in the confirmation email so no one is surprised. Who: Zoe. Measured by: the rule is in every confirmation email.

Pilot-in-the-plane: check and adjust every week

Fly, look at the instruments, adjust, and fly again. The weekly review is the habit that makes the rest work.

  1. Hold a 20-minute review every week. Same day each week (Sunday works). Answer four questions: how many did we contact, how many replied, what is booked, what one thing will we change next week? Who: the whole team. Measured by: a dated entry in the tracker Notes for each review.
  2. Stop what is not working. If a message gets no reply after two rounds, mark it "no reply" and move to another partner. Who: whoever sent it. Measured by: the count of "no reply" rows, and new messages sent to replace them.

Ways to stand out

Plenty of people can teach a cooking class. What makes this one yours is the design work: you test ideas, see what happens, and change course. Below are six angles to test this month. Each one is cheap enough to try once. The farm angle is not yet proven, so treat it as a test: start with one question to Morven (template d) before you build anything around it.

1. First-apartment survival

Pitch: One class ends with a week of meals and a grocery list under a set budget, so a second-year can shop and cook alone.

Why UVA students: Second-years move into their first apartment and have never cooked for themselves.

Cheapest test: Run one pilot class with a friend group and hand out a printed grocery list and budget sheet. Cost: one class of groceries.

Metric: How many feedback answers say they used the list, and how many book a second class.

2. Roommates' apartment night

Pitch: Book one class for the whole apartment. Roommates cook together in the kitchen they already share.

Why UVA students: Roommates already share groceries, meals and a kitchen, so the group is already built.

Cheapest test: Offer it to one roommate group, and ask them to invite a neighboring apartment if they are short of 6.

Metric: Roommate-group bookings, and how many people each group brings.

3. Sisterhood or club cook-off

Pitch: A team cook-off for a chapter or club. Teams get a theme, and everyone takes their meals home. Price stays $14 a person.

Why UVA students: Chapters and clubs look for shared activities to plan together.

Cheapest test: Offer the team format to one chapter or club you already know.

Metric: The booking itself, and whether the group asks to rebook.

4. Gluten-free done right

Pitch: A class where gluten-free cooking is taught properly, with every recipe checked, not treated as an afterthought.

Why UVA students: Lauren has celiac disease, so she can teach gluten-free cooking from real experience.

Cheapest test: Run one class labeled gluten-free with two recipes Lauren has already tested.

Metric: Booking requests that mention dietary needs, and what people say about it in the feedback form.

5. The parent gift

Pitch: A class parents can book for a student as "a night off from the dining hall."

Why UVA students: Parents in UVA groups already talk about their students' first years. The flyer is built for this.

Cheapest test: Post template (f) in one UVA parents group. Check the group's posting rules first.

Metric: Parent inquiries, and bookings a parent makes for a student.

6. Pay-it-forward you can see

Pitch: Show where the money goes. Every confirmation says what part of the class goes to the UVA Community Food Pantry, and the site shows a running total once there is a real one.

Why UVA students: Students want to know that their money does something useful.

Cheapest test: Add one line to the confirmation email, and ask in the feedback form whether it mattered to them.

Metric: How many feedback answers mention the pantry, and whether the running total is shown with real figures.

Show your design process

Your professor wants to see that you learned, not only that you launched. Keep a Design log, a Google Doc or shared note with one entry per idea you test. Each entry has four lines: Hypothesis (what you believed), Test (what you did, as cheaply as you could), Result (what happened, with real counts), and What changed (what you did next, including ideas you dropped).

Write only what you saw. A no is a result, and a dropped idea is part of the process.

Example entries, using what is true so far:

Entry 1: Price

Hypothesis: Students will pay about the price the survey suggested.

Test: A price survey.

Result: The survey suggested $10 to $15.

What changed: We set $14 a person for groups of 6 or more.

Entry 2: Venue

Hypothesis: A class works in a student's apartment kitchen.

Test: Our first class, held in an apartment kitchen.

Result: [Fill in what happened: how many people came, what went well, what broke.]

What changed: [Fill in after the class.]

Entry 3: Farm partner

Hypothesis: Morven Kitchen Garden can supply produce for classes.

Test: Send template (d) and ask about produce directly.

Result: [Pending. Write down their answer, or note that there was no reply.]

What changed: [Pending. If the answer is no, drop the idea and log why.]

Send faster

  • Send from your @virginia.edu account. Partners see a UVA student, and that is the point. Use your name and a real signature line.
  • Keep a separate contacts sheet for mail merge. The tracker has no email column on purpose, so people's addresses stay out of the shared log. Make a second sheet with Name, Email, Organization, and Type. Merge from that sheet, then log each send in tracker.csv.
  • Mail merge in Word with Outlook. Open a blank Word document, go to Mailings, then Start Mail Merge, then E-mail Messages. Choose Select Recipients, Use an Existing List, and pick your contacts sheet. Use Insert Merge Field for the name, then send from Outlook. Test with one address you control first.
  • Mail merge in Gmail. If your account is Gmail, look at YAMM (Yet Another Mail Merge) or GMass. Both have free tiers. Check current limits before a big send, since free limits can change.
  • Check your account's bulk-sending rules. If you're unsure whether your UVA account allows bulk email, ask UVA IT before you send a large batch.
  • Personalize the first line. Mention something real about the group (their philanthropy, their last event). A merged email that reads like a form gets ignored. Send in small batches of 5 to 10 a day.
  • Where to find contacts.
    • Clubs and CIOs: the student organization directory on UVA's student affairs pages. Search "UVA student organizations" to find the current list.
    • Sororities and fraternities: look for the Panhellenic and IFC chapter pages, which list chapters. If you can't find them, ask Greek life. Chapter contacts often include a social or philanthropy chair.
    • Residence life: the housing and residence life site lists hall staff. Ask for the RA for your building if you can't find a name.
    • Parents: search for UVA parent groups on Facebook. Read the group rules before posting.

Track it for your professor

Every message goes in tracker.csv. It starts empty. Add one row per person you contact, and update the Status and dates as things change.

What each column means

  • Date contacted: the day you sent the message (YYYY-MM-DD).
  • Name: the person you contacted.
  • Organization: their group, hall, partner or chapter.
  • Type: sorority, club, RA, partner, parent, or other.
  • Channel: email, DM, GroupMe, or in person.
  • Template used: the letter from this page, such as (a) or (j). Write "custom" if you wrote it yourself.
  • Status: sent, replied, booked, declined, or no reply.
  • Follow-up date: when you plan to check back.
  • Class date: the date of the class, once booked.
  • Group size: how many people are in the group.
  • Notes: what they said, the cap you set, and the weekly review entries.

The five numbers to report

  1. Contacts reached: the number of rows with a Date contacted.
  2. Replies: rows with Status replied, booked or declined, divided by contacts reached.
  3. Classes booked: rows with Status booked and a Class date.
  4. People taught: the total Group size for classes that have already happened.
  5. Dollars to the UVA Community Food Pantry: people taught, times the per-person amount you agreed on with the pantry. Use the figure you agreed on, not a guess.

Report what the feedback forms say as they are written. A small number of responses is fine. Say how many responses there were.
